Public discussions about technology often move between two extremes: the expectation that innovation will solve longstanding problems and the fear that it will inevitably make them worse. Both responses are understandable, yet neither leaves much room for examining the conditions under which a particular tool succeeds or fails. Consider an automated system introduced to improve access to a public service. Its value depends not only on technical accuracy, but also on who can use it, how mistakes are challenged, and whether the people affected have any influence over its design. A more productive debate would therefore focus less on technology in the abstract and more on the institutions, incentives, and human choices that shape its consequences.
