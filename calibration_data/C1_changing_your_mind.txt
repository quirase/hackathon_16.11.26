I used to think that changing your mind was a sign of uncertainty. If I had expressed an opinion confidently, I felt obliged to defend it, even when someone presented a reasonable objection. Looking back, I suspect I was more concerned about appearing consistent than about reaching an accurate conclusion.

That attitude began to change during a project at work. Our team was deciding how to improve a service, and I strongly supported adding several new features. My assumption was that people would use the service more often if they had more options. A colleague suggested that we speak to existing users before making a decision. I agreed, although I expected the interviews to confirm what I already believed.

Instead, we discovered that many users were struggling with the features we already had. They were not asking for more choices; they wanted the existing process to be easier to understand. The evidence didn’t mean that new features would never be useful, but it did suggest that our immediate priority should be different.

Changing direction was initially uncomfortable, partly because I had invested time in the original proposal. Yet once we focused on the actual problem, the discussion became much more constructive. We stopped treating the choice as a contest between two people and started examining what would best serve the users.

Since then, I’ve tried to be clearer about the difference between confidence and certainty. It’s possible to make a strong recommendation based on the information available while remaining open to revising it. In fact, I now see that willingness as part of responsible decision-making rather than a weakness.
